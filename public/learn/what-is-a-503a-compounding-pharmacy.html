What is a 503A compounding pharmacy, and how is it different from 503B?

Last updated November 13, 2026. Written by Velacore Health and not reviewed by a clinician. General education, not medical advice.

A 503A compounding pharmacy is a state-licensed pharmacy that prepares a medication for one named patient after it receives a valid prescription for that patient. A 503B outsourcing facility is a different category that registers with the FDA and follows manufacturing rules. The two are named after sections 503A and 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.

What 503A means

The FDA describes compounding as a practice in which a licensed pharmacist or a licensed physician combines, mixes or alters ingredients of a drug to create a medication tailored to the needs of an individual patient.

Section 503A of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act sets the conditions under which a compounded drug can be made without going through the FDA approval process. Under 503A, the compounding is done by a licensed pharmacist in a state-licensed pharmacy or a federal facility, or by a licensed physician.

A drug made under 503A is exempt from three federal requirements: FDA approval before marketing, current good manufacturing practice rules, and labeling with adequate directions for use. Those exemptions apply only when the conditions of the section are met.

What patient-specific prescription means

The central condition of 503A is the prescription. The FDA states that drugs under this section must be compounded based on the receipt of valid patient-specific prescriptions.

In plain terms, the pharmacy prepares the medication for one named person, after a licensed prescriber has written a prescription for that person. A 503A pharmacy does not make a batch for general sale and then look for buyers.

This is why the order of events matters. The clinician reviews the patient and decides first. The pharmacy fills second.

What a 503B outsourcing facility is

Section 503B was added in 2013 by the Drug Quality and Security Act. It created a voluntary category of compounder called an outsourcing facility.

The law defines an outsourcing facility as a facility at one geographic location or address that is engaged in the compounding of sterile drugs and has chosen to register with the FDA.

An outsourcing facility may distribute compounded drugs under a patient-specific prescription. It may also fill an order from a health care provider, such as a hospital, that is not for an identified individual patient. That second route is often called office stock.

How 503A and 503B differ

  • Prescription. 503A requires a valid prescription for an individual patient. 503B can also supply health care providers without one.
  • Manufacturing rules. 503A pharmacies are exempt from current good manufacturing practice requirements. 503B outsourcing facilities must comply with them.
  • Registration. 503A pharmacies are licensed by their state. 503B facilities register with the FDA.
  • Reporting. 503B facilities report adverse events to the FDA and send it a list of the drugs they compounded every six months.

One thing is the same for both. Compounded medications are not FDA approved and are not reviewed by the FDA for safety or efficacy. That is true whether the drug comes from a 503A pharmacy or a 503B outsourcing facility.

Who regulates each

State boards of pharmacy have primary responsibility for the day-to-day oversight of state-licensed pharmacies, which includes 503A pharmacies. The FDA also carries out surveillance and for-cause inspections of these pharmacies.

Outsourcing facilities registered under 503B are overseen primarily by the FDA, which inspects them on a risk-based schedule.

So the short answer to who regulates a 503A pharmacy is the state first, with the FDA in a supporting role. For a 503B outsourcing facility, it is the FDA first.

Common questions

Is a 503A pharmacy regulated by the FDA?

Mostly by its state. State boards of pharmacy have primary responsibility for day-to-day oversight, and the FDA carries out surveillance and for-cause inspections.

Does a 503A pharmacy need a prescription for each patient?

Yes. Drugs under section 503A must be compounded based on a valid patient-specific prescription.

Are medications from a 503B facility FDA approved?

No. The FDA states that compounded drugs are not FDA approved, and that covers both categories.

Which type of pharmacy fills Velacore prescriptions?

Prescriptions written by the independent clinicians are filled by licensed 503A compounding pharmacies. Every program is prescription only.
