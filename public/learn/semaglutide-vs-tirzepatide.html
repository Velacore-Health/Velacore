Semaglutide and tirzepatide: how the two differ

Last updated November 13, 2026. Written by Velacore Health and not reviewed by a clinician. General education, not medical advice.

Semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which means it acts on one hormone receptor. Tirzepatide is a dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist, which means it acts on two. Both are prescription only, and a licensed clinician decides whether either one is appropriate.

What GLP-1 and GIP are

GLP-1 stands for glucagon-like peptide-1. GIP stands for glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide. Both are hormones made in the intestine.

A receptor agonist is a medication that activates a hormone's receptor. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases puts it in plainer words: the medication mimics the hormone.

Both receptors are part of pathways involved in blood sugar regulation and appetite signaling. This article describes what each medication acts on. It does not describe what either one will do for a particular person.

How semaglutide works

Semaglutide is a peptide that acts on the GLP-1 receptor. It mimics one hormone, GLP-1. That single target is why it is called a GLP-1 receptor agonist.

The FDA lists semaglutide among the GLP-1 receptor agonist medications.

In the Semaglutide program, it is prepared as an injection given under the skin. There are five dose levels. The clinician starts at the lowest level and reviews an update from you each month before any increase.

How tirzepatide works

Tirzepatide is a peptide that acts on two receptors, GIP and GLP-1. It mimics two hormones instead of one. That is why it is called a dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist.

In the Tirzepatide program, it is also prepared as an injection given under the skin. It has five dose levels as well, with the same monthly review before any increase.

So the difference is the number of receptors. Semaglutide acts on one, the GLP-1 receptor. Tirzepatide acts on two, the GIP receptor and the GLP-1 receptor. They are two different medications, and each needs its own prescription.

What the two have in common

  • Both are peptides.
  • Both act on the GLP-1 receptor.
  • Both are given by injection under the skin. In our programs the clinician writes the schedule on your prescription.
  • Both are prescription only.
  • In our programs, both include baseline labs at a national laboratory, shipping and clinician reviews in the program price.

The compounded versions in our programs are prepared by a licensed 503A compounding pharmacy to your clinician's prescription. Compounded medications are not FDA approved and are not reviewed by the FDA for safety or efficacy.

What this article does not compare

This article covers mechanism only. It does not say which medication is better, and it does not compare how people respond to them. Acting on two receptors is a description of how a medication works. It is not a statement that one is stronger or more suitable than the other.

Which medication fits a person is a clinical question. It depends on health history, current medications and other things only a clinician can weigh.

Knowing the mechanism is useful background for a conversation with a clinician. It is not a way to choose a medication on your own.

The clinician decides which one, if either

The licensed clinician makes the prescribing decision. You choose the program you want to start with and complete an online intake about your health, history and medications. A clinician licensed in your state reviews it and decides which program, if any, is appropriate.

Both programs are for adults 18 to 74 with a body mass index of 27 or above, or 25 or above with a related health condition. A video visit is available on request at no extra cost, and it is required for the first prescription in a few states.

For both programs, the clinician reviews your progress each month until your dose is steady, then quarterly. Refills ship every four weeks.

If the clinician does not prescribe, nothing is ordered and your program payment is refunded in full. Velacore coordinates access and education. It does not prescribe.

Common questions

Is tirzepatide a GLP-1?

It acts on the GLP-1 receptor and also on the GIP receptor. That is why it is described as a dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist.

Are both given as injections?

In our programs, yes. Both are prepared as an injection given under the skin.

Which one is better?

We do not make that comparison. The licensed clinician decides which program, if any, is appropriate for you.

Do I need a prescription for either one?

Yes. Both are prescription only.
