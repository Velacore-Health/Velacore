## 1. LegitScript

### What the standards say
LegitScript's healthcare certification has nine standards. The ones that matter for these two categories [platform policy, <https://www.legitscript.com/certification/healthcare-certification/>, read Nov 18 2026]:
- Standard 2, legal compliance. A certified site "must not facilitate the prescribing or dispensing of medications" that lack the needed approval or are "otherwise considered unapproved by relevant regulatory bodies."
- Standard 4, affiliates and partners. All domains and affiliates must be disclosed, and essential partners such as the fulfilling pharmacy generally need their own certification or accreditation.
- Standard 7, valid prescription. Medication only on a valid prescription, never before a licensed professional has provided care, and state telemedicine rules apply.
- Standard 8, transparency. No claims of benefits "that have not been supported by a regulatory body."
- Standard 9, advertising. Ads must be accurate and not misleading, and ads that break platform rules can be grounds for denial or revocation.

The published standards do not mention compounded drugs, sexual health or hormones by name. There is no separate ED or HRT standard.

### Does it add anything beyond what we already meet
Mostly the same rules, applied to new products. What is new in practice:
1. The pharmacy. Whatever pharmacy fills the troches, the vials and the estradiol must be licensed in every state served and, under Standard 4, generally certified or accredited itself. We have not been told which pharmacy OpenLoop uses for these categories. [unconfirmed]
2. The product list in the application. OpenLoop wrote in Jan 2025 that sellers of compounded medications fill out a spreadsheet for each product covering quality assurance and patient safety. [vendor claim, unconfirmed, <https://openloophealth.com/blog/how-to-get-legitscript-certified-and-why-its-important>] So each sexual health and HRT item likely needs its own row. Decide the categories before we file, so the application covers them from day one.
3. Whether adding a category after certification needs a fresh review or a notice to LegitScript is not stated on the public page. [unconfirmed] Ask LegitScript or the consultant.
4. The 21 and over age gate for sexual health must actually be enforced in OpenLoop's intake, because Standard 7 ties every prescription to the jurisdiction's rules.

### Compounded ED products compared with FDA-approved generics
Yes, they are treated differently, and the difference is real risk for the compounded ones.
- Tadalafil is sold as an FDA-approved generic and is not in shortage. Federal law does not let a 503A pharmacy regularly compound drugs that are essentially copies of a commercially available drug. A compounded product escapes that only when the prescriber documents a change that makes a significant difference for that patient. [regulator record, FDA guidance on essentially copies, Jan 2018, <https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/compounded-drug-products-are-essentially-copies-commercially-available-drug-product-under-section>]
- LegitScript says the same thing in its own words: compounded drugs cannot be essentially copies unless a meaningful clinical difference is clearly shown, and lower price or convenience does not count. [platform policy, LegitScript blog, May 19 2025, <https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/from-shortage-heroes-to-compliance-champions-what-todays-glp-1-pharmacies-must-know/>]
- In Sep 2026 the FDA warned a large 503A pharmacy that its products looked like essentially copies compounded regularly, and treated a high volume of one formulation as a sign the stated difference was pretext. [secondary, Sep 2026, <https://www.bipc.com/fda-warning-letter-to-empower-pharmacy-and-what-it-means-for-503a-compounding-pharmacies>] That letter was about GLP-1s, but the same test applies to tadalafil.
- A troche or a combination is a different form or formula, which is the usual basis for the clinical-difference argument. Whether it holds is the prescriber's and pharmacy's call, not ours, and LegitScript can look at it under Standard 2. [unconfirmed, my reading]
- An FDA-approved generic tablet carries none of these compounding questions. If the daily tadalafil 28 pack is an approved generic, it is the lowest-risk item in the category. Ask OpenLoop which it is.

### Women's HRT and LegitScript
Same nine standards. Estradiol is not a controlled substance, so no DEA question arises. The same pharmacy and product-row points apply. Compounded hormones carry a known quality concern, covered in section 4, which is one more reason to confirm whether OpenLoop's estradiol is compounded or an approved product.

## 2. Meta

### Sexual health
- Prescription drug ads need active LegitScript certification, then a separate authorization request to Meta. US, Canada or New Zealand only, and only people 18 or older. [platform policy, Drugs and Pharmaceuticals, current version, <https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drugs-pharmaceuticals/>]
- Meta's adult products and reproductive health standard allows sexual health ads "as long as the focus is on health and the medical efficacy of the product or the service," lists prevention of erectile dysfunction as allowed, bans a focus on sexual pleasure or enhancement, and requires 18 or older targeting. [platform policy, <https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/content-specific-restrictions/adult-products-or-services>, versions dated Jul 23 2026 and current]
- Meta's personal attributes rule bans ads that assert or imply a viewer's "physical or mental health," including medical conditions, or their "sexual orientation or practices," and bans implying knowledge of a viewer's medical information. [platform policy, <https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/>] So "Struggling with ED?" fails. "Licensed clinicians review sexual health requests online" passes the attribute test.
- Nudity and suggestive imagery fall under Meta's adult nudity and sexual activity standard, which applies to every ad. [platform policy, <https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/adult-nudity-and-sexual-activity/>]
- Our own rule, stricter than Meta: target 21 and older on every sexual health ad set, because OpenLoop's eligibility is 21 and over. Showing the ad to 18 to 20 year olds would pay for clicks that can never become patients.

### Women's HRT
- Estradiol is a prescription drug, so the same LegitScript plus Meta authorization route applies. [platform policy, Drugs and Pharmaceuticals, as above]
- Meta's health and wellness standard lists "the effects of menopause" as an allowed reproductive health topic for people 18 and older. [platform policy, <https://transparency.meta.com/en-gb/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness>, versions dated 22 Jul 2026 and current]
- The Jul 22 2026 version of that page bans ads that "imply or attempt to generate negative self-perception." Fatigue, mood and aging imagery aimed at making a woman feel bad about herself fails.
- Personal attributes apply here too. "Going through menopause?" fails. Talk about the service, never the viewer.

### Tracking on Meta for both categories
Since early 2025 Meta limits which conversion events health advertisers can optimize to, and it disables custom audiences, lookalikes and custom conversions whose names point to a health condition. [secondary, Triple Whale and Accelerated Digital Media, 2025 to 2026, <https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/meta-health-and-wellness-brands>, <https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/health-marketing-policy-updates-2025/>, Meta's own help page not checked, unconfirmed]
What that means for us: our only measurable conversion is the Start visit click, and Meta may restrict even that for these pages. Event names, campaign names, UTM values and audience names must stay neutral, with no condition or drug words in them. Reconcile against OpenLoop's weekly payout counts, as with every program.

## 3. Google Ads

### Sexual health
- Google's sexual health and wellness policy allows prescription drugs for erectile dysfunction, and for female sexual interest and arousal disorder, only in Canada, New Zealand and the United States, and only after the online pharmacy or telemedicine provider applies and is approved. Ads cannot focus on sexual pleasure or enhancement, cannot use sexually suggestive elements, must target only users 18 or older, and must follow local law. [platform policy, <https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15275138>, no date on page, read Nov 18 2026]
- That page says these products may run "on the Google Display Network and YouTube in limited cases." It does not mention Search. How Search ads for ED are served is governed by the healthcare and medicines policy, and the exact serving limits on Search are [unconfirmed]. Ask Google certification support before planning a Search budget for this category.
- US telemedicine providers must hold LegitScript's Healthcare Merchant Certification and then be certified by Google. [platform policy, <https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031>]
- Keywords. Since Oct 29 2025, US advertisers may use prescription drug terms in ad text and landing pages without certification, but must be certified to bid on keywords containing them, and telemedicine providers need certification regardless. [platform policy, update notice, <https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16606361>] So bidding on "tadalafil" is only possible after both certifications.
- Country. ED drug ads are allowed only in the US, Canada and New Zealand. We serve the US only, so target United States only.

### Women's HRT
- Google's sexual content policy names hormone replacement therapy as allowed, "even with possible secondary sexual benefits," so HRT is not treated as sexual content. [platform policy, <https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6023699>]
- Estradiol is still a prescription drug term, so the same LegitScript plus Google certification route and the same keyword rule apply.

## 4. FDA and FTC basics

### FDA, compounded products
- Compounded drugs are not FDA approved. A compounded drug is misbranded if its advertising or promotion is false or misleading, under section 502, paragraph bb, of the federal drug law. [regulator record, FDA warning letter to an ED telehealth brand, Sep 9 2025, <https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/dermacare-llc-dba-bluechew-716698-09092025>]
- In that letter the FDA objected to saying a compounded ED product has the same active ingredient as a named brand pill, and to "work for most patients." It said these claims "imply that your products are the same as an FDA-approved product when they are not," and added that overstating efficacy or minimizing risk "may constitute misbranding."
- A second Sep 9 2025 letter to another ED brand objected to "the exact same active ingredients as the branded variety for less" and to promoting "high concentrations" in a two-ingredient product. [regulator record, <https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/hello-cake-inc-dba-hello-cake-716484-09092025>]
- More letter waves followed: about 80 warning and 40 untitled letters in Sep 2025, 30 in Mar 2026, 25 in Jun 2026, some covering compounded ED products. [secondary, <https://natlawreview.com/article/fdas-focus-returns-compounding-and-telehealth-another-wave-warning-letters>, Jun 2026]
- The Mar 2026 wave objected to calling compounded drugs generic equivalents, to implying clinical trial testing, and to showing compounded products "seemingly branded with the telehealth firm's name or trademark, implying the telehealth company was the compounder." [secondary, Frier Levitt, Mar 9 2026, <https://www.frierlevitt.com/articles/fda-telehealth-advertising-compounded-glp1-compliance/>]

That last point reaches past this category. Our vial images carry the Velacore label. The site already says the images are illustrations and that the pharmacy prepares and labels the medication, which helps. Whether that is enough is a question for the attorney and vc-reviewer, and it applies to every program, not just these two.

### FDA, estradiol and the boxed warning
- On Nov 10 2025 the FDA announced it would remove the cardiovascular, breast cancer and probable dementia statements from the boxed warning on menopausal hormone therapy labels. The endometrial cancer warning for estrogen-alone systemic products stays. [secondary, Harvard Health and RAPS, <https://www.health.harvard.edu/womens-health/fda-panel-reviews-menopause-hormone-therapy-warnings>, <https://www.raps.org/resource/fda-to-remove-boxed-warnings-from-hormone-therapie.html>]
- The change is product by product and not finished. Six products were relabeled in Feb 2026 and three estradiol patches on Oct 30 2026; 29 companies had submitted changes. [secondary, Contemporary OB/GYN, Oct 30 2026, <https://www.contemporaryobgyn.net/view/fda-removes-boxed-warnings-from-3-more-menopausal-hormone-therapy-products>]
- A compounded estradiol has no FDA label at all, so it has no boxed warning to remove. The FDA's change says nothing about compounded hormones being safer.
- What the site should do: carry an important safety information block for estradiol, written or approved in writing by OpenLoop's clinical team, never by us. It should name the serious risks, say a clinician decides, and link to the full information. Whether FDA's formal fair balance rules for drug advertising bind a telehealth platform is contested [secondary, Bloomberg Law, <https://news.bloomberglaw.com/health-law-and-business/fda-signals-more-oversight-of-compounders-in-drug-ads-crackdown>], and the FDA's own letters treat minimizing risk as misbranding. Plan as if they apply.
- The FDA's consumer page on bioidentical hormones says it knows of no credible evidence that compounded bioidentical hormones are safer or more effective, and FDA acted against seven compounders for such claims in 2008. "Bioidentical" is a marketing word the FDA does not recognize. [regulator record, archived FDA consumer update, Apr 2008, <https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20080916230938/http://www.fda.gov/consumer/updates/bioidenticals040808.pdf>; the Menopause Society, <https://menopause.org/publications/clinical-practice-materials/bioidentical-hormone-therapy/us-government-takes-action>]

### FTC
- Any health claim needs competent and reliable scientific evidence, usually randomized controlled trials. Testimonials, traditional use and observational studies do not count. [regulator record, FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance, Dec 2022, <https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance>] We make no outcome claims, so this mostly bites if a partner or influencer does.
- Subscriptions. The FTC's click-to-cancel rule was vacated by the Eighth Circuit in Jul 2025, but ROSCA, the FTC Act and state auto-renewal laws still apply. [secondary, Fenwick, Jul 2025, <https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/eighth-circuit-vacates-ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule>]
- Jul 2025, a GLP-1 telehealth firm settled FTC charges over misleading prices, fake reviews and hard cancellation; it must refund customers and stop incentivized reviews. [regulator record via secondary, <https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2025/08/health-care-marketing-enforcement-ftc-targets-telehealth-company-for-deceptive-w/>]
- Jul 29 2026, the FTC with Utah and Los Angeles County sued a large telehealth brand that sells sexual health treatments. Alleged, not decided: people were told they would not be charged until a medication was prescribed, yet were billed and enrolled without a provider consult; refill timing was unclear; cancellation was buried; and pixels and customer lists sent health browsing, including ED treatment data, to ad platforms against the privacy policy. [secondary, Alston and Bird, Aug 10 2026, <https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/ftc-lawsuit-hims-hers-rosca>; Holland and Knight, Aug 25 2026, <https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/the-pixel-problem-advertising-triggers-health-data-obligations>]
- Washington's My Health My Data Act treats health browsing as consumer health data and lets individuals sue; ad pixels on treatment pages are the main exposure. [secondary, <https://thenai.org/washington-my-health-my-data-act/>] Sexual health and hormone pages are the most sensitive pages we would ever host.

What this means for us: before either category goes live, the attorney decides whether ad pixels may fire on these pages at all, whether a consent banner is needed first, and whether our privacy policy matches what GA4 and PostHog actually collect. The fake reviews rule, finalized Aug 2024, also applies; I did not recheck it today. [unconfirmed]

## 5. Competitor pitfalls, by pattern

### Sexual health
1. Brand sameness. "Same active ingredient as" a named brand, "generic" for a compounded product, a brand name next to our product. FDA warning letters, Sep 2025.
2. Speed, duration and success claims. "Works for most men", "in 15 minutes", "lasts all weekend", "stronger than the pill". Efficacy claims, and the "high concentrations" line drew an FDA letter.
3. Suggestive creative. Innuendo, fruit and vegetable emojis, bedroom couples, "perform", "last longer", "rise". Fails Meta and Google on pleasure focus.
4. Skip-the-doctor framing. "No doctor visit", "approved in minutes", "no questions asked". Breaks LegitScript Standard 7 and our model.
5. House-branded compounded products that imply the telehealth brand makes the drug. FDA, Mar 2026.
6. Teaser pricing. "From $X" that only holds for a large prepaid pack, per-dose prices hiding the pack price, auto-refill not stated next to the button. FTC pattern.
7. Charging before clinician review, or saying no charge until prescribed and then charging. Alleged in the Jul 2026 complaint.
8. Pixels on ED pages sending page views or customer lists to ad platforms. Alleged in the Jul 2026 complaint.
9. Written-for-us reviews or paid reviews without disclosure. FTC.
10. Targeting under 21 when eligibility is 21. Wasted spend, and a mismatch LegitScript can notice.

### Women's HRT
1. "Bioidentical means natural and safer." FDA position since 2008.
2. Using the Nov 2025 FDA boxed warning change as a safety claim, or applying it to compounded hormones that never had an FDA label.
3. Symptom promises. "End hot flashes", "sleep through the night again", "get your libido back", "feel like yourself". Efficacy claims.
4. Prevention claims. Heart, bone, brain, memory or anti-aging benefits. Disease claims that need trials.
5. No safety information, or safety buried under the button.
6. "$79 a month" without "billed $237 every 12 weeks" next to it. Same FTC pricing pattern.
7. Labs presented as included when the patient pays. The proposal says HRT labs are patient-paid.
8. Ad copy that names the viewer's stage of life or symptoms. Meta personal attributes and negative self-perception rules.
